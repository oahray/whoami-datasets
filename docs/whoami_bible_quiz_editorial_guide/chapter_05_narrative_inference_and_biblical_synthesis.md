# 5. Narrative Inference and Biblical Synthesis

Some of the most rewarding clues in a WhoAmI dataset are not stated explicitly in Scripture.

Instead, they are conclusions that naturally emerge from reading multiple passages together.

These clues often become players' favourites because they reward careful study rather than simple memorisation.

However, this editorial approach requires discipline.

A contributor must distinguish between:

- **reasonable biblical conclusions**, and
- **interpretations that go beyond what Scripture supports.**

This chapter defines where that line should be drawn.

---



# The Golden Rule

A clue may be inferred **only if the cited passages lead to a single reasonable conclusion.**

If multiple reasonable conclusions exist, the clue should be rewritten or discarded.

This is the single most important principle governing narrative inference.

---



# Acceptable Inference

Good inference simply connects facts that Scripture already provides.

The conclusion should feel inevitable once the reader examines the cited passages.

For example:

> I began the journey to Canaan, but my son completed it.

No verse states this sentence verbatim.

However:

- Terah left Ur.
- Terah travelled toward Canaan.
- Terah stopped in Haran.
- Abraham later completed the journey.

The conclusion follows naturally from the narrative.

No speculation is required.

---

Another example:

> Abraham was the ninth generation in line from me.

Scripture records every generation.

The contributor simply counts them.

Again, no interpretation has been added.

---

Another example:

> I was still alive when Sarah died.

Genesis provides:

- Shem's lifespan.
- Abraham's age.
- Sarah's age.

Combining those passages produces a single chronological conclusion.

No external chronology is needed.

---



# Unacceptable Inference

Some conclusions appear reasonable but are not demanded by the biblical text.

These should not be used as clues.

For example:

> I missed the ten plagues.

This seems likely for Zipporah.

However, Scripture never states exactly when Moses sent her back to Midian.

The most common interpretation may be correct.

It is not the only reasonable interpretation.

Therefore the clue should not be used.

---

Another example:

> I saved my husband's life.

The encounter at the lodging place in Exodus 4 is intentionally ambiguous.

Readers disagree about:

- who Jehovah intended to strike,
- why,
- and how Zipporah's actions resolved the situation.

Because the passage supports multiple serious interpretations, the clue introduces unnecessary uncertainty.

A stronger clue simply describes what the text explicitly records.

For example:

> I circumcised my son with a flint.

or

> I called someone a bridegroom of blood.

---



# Synthesis Is Not Interpretation

Contributors sometimes avoid synthesis because they fear adding to Scripture.

This concern is understandable.

However, synthesis and interpretation are not the same thing.

Synthesis combines biblical facts.

Interpretation explains what those facts mean.

For example:

> Abraham was the ninth generation in line from me.

This is synthesis.

Nothing theological has been added.

By contrast:

> I represented God's covenant faithfulness.

This is interpretation.

Different readers may understand the passage differently.

Such clues should generally be avoided.

---



# Chronology

Chronological reasoning is encouraged.

Scripture frequently provides:

- ages
- lifespans
- reign lengths
- periods of time

These allow contributors to derive observations that are not explicitly written.

Whenever chronology is used:

- cite every relevant passage;
- ensure the calculation can be reproduced;
- avoid relying on external chronological systems.

---



# Genealogy

Genealogical reasoning is also encouraged.

Scripture often records family lines in remarkable detail.

These genealogies can produce excellent Nightmare clues.

Always ensure that:

- every relationship is cited;
- every generation can be verified;
- no missing generations are assumed.

The player should be able to reconstruct the genealogy directly from Scripture.

---



# Narrative Observation

Sometimes Scripture communicates facts through the flow of the story rather than through explicit statements.

These observations may be used when the narrative clearly supports them.

Examples include:

- who travelled together;
- who witnessed an event;
- who arrived after another event;
- who inherited responsibility.

Again, ask:

> Does the narrative leave room for another reasonable conclusion?

If the answer is yes, avoid the clue.

---



# The Editorial Test

Whenever writing a synthesis clue, ask:

> Could another careful Bible reader, using only the cited passages, reasonably disagree with this conclusion?

If the answer is yes, the clue should probably be rewritten or removed.

If the answer is no, the clue is usually suitable for inclusion.

This standard protects both the accuracy of the dataset and the trust of its players.

---



## Editorial Insight

One of the defining characteristics of the WhoAmI datasets is that they reward readers who connect Scripture together.

The objective is not to surprise players with hidden information.

The objective is to help them discover relationships that were always present in the biblical text.

When players reveal the answer, the ideal reaction is:

> "I never noticed that before."

not

> "How was I supposed to know that?"

That distinction separates rewarding discovery from frustrating trivia.

***The contributor bears the burden of proving that a clue is correct, not the reviewer bearing the burden of disproving it.***

---



## Key Takeaways

- Inference is allowed only when Scripture supports a single reasonable conclusion.
- Synthesis combines biblical facts; interpretation adds meaning.
- Chronology and genealogy often produce excellent Nightmare clues.
- Avoid clues based on disputed passages or uncertain assumptions.
- When in doubt, stay closer to the wording and flow of Scripture.

# 8. Common Editorial Pitfalls

Writing good clues is only half the editorial process.

Recognising weak clues is equally important.

This chapter highlights the mistakes contributors make most often, together with examples of how they can be improved.

Many of these examples come directly from real editorial reviews during the development of the WhoAmI datasets.

---

# Pitfall 1: Assuming Instead of Demonstrating

One of the easiest mistakes is writing a clue that feels true without confirming that Scripture actually supports it.

Example:

> I missed the ten plagues.

This appears reasonable for Zipporah.

However, Scripture never states exactly when Moses sent her back to Midian.

Several timelines remain possible.

Better:

> I was the daughter of Jethro.

or

> I called someone a bridegroom of blood.

Always demonstrate a conclusion from Scripture rather than assuming it.

---



# Pitfall 2: Building on Ambiguous Passages

Some passages intentionally leave room for interpretation.

Avoid building clues on disputed details.

Example:

> I saved my husband's life.

Exodus 4 does not clearly explain every aspect of the incident.

Different readers understand the passage differently.

Better:

> I circumcised my son with a flint.

This describes an undisputed event rather than an interpretation.

---



# Pitfall 3: Confusing Synthesis with Speculation

Good synthesis combines biblical facts.

Speculation adds information that Scripture never establishes.

Good:

> Abraham was the ninth generation in line from me.

Poor:

> I knew Abraham personally.

Although both may appear related, only the first can be demonstrated directly from Scripture.

Always ask whether the conclusion is supported or merely imagined.

---



# Pitfall 4: Making Clues Too Generic

Generic clues leave too many possible answers.

Weak:

> I had children.

Better:

> I had five daughters.

Weak:

> I travelled.

Better:

> I fled from Egypt to Midian.

Specific clues create stronger gameplay.

---



# Pitfall 5: Repeating the Same Information

Every clue should contribute something new.

Poor:

> I became the father of Isaac.

> Isaac was my son.

These communicate the same idea.

Replace one of them with another distinctive fact.

---



# Pitfall 6: Confusing Difficulty with Obscurity

Not every obscure fact produces a good Nightmare clue.

Weak:

> I owned a particular field.

Although obscure, this fact contributes little to identifying the character.

Better Nightmare clues require reasoning rather than hidden information.

Ask:

> Does this clue reward biblical understanding?

or merely

> Does almost nobody remember this verse?

The first creates better gameplay.

---



# Pitfall 7: Giving the Answer Too Early

Some clues immediately identify the character.

That is appropriate for Easy clues.

It is not appropriate for Hard or Nightmare clues.

***Poor progression:***

Easy:

> I became king after Saul.

Nightmare:

> I defeated Goliath.

***Better progression:***

Easy:

> I defeated Goliath.

Medium:

> I became the second king of Israel.

Hard:

> I spared my enemy inside a cave.

Nightmare:

> I refused to harm Jehovah's anointed.

Difficulty should increase—not decrease.

---



# Pitfall 8: Using Interpretation as Fact

Avoid describing theological conclusions as though they were explicit biblical statements.

Weak:

> I represented God's covenant faithfulness.

Better:

> Jehovah changed my name.

The first interprets.

The second describes.

Editorial neutrality requires contributors to favour observable biblical facts.

---



# Pitfall 9: Poor Citation Choices

Choosing an unnecessarily broad or indirect citation slows the review process.

Weak:

Genesis 5–9

Better:

Genesis 6:14

Always cite the passage that most directly supports the clue.

---



# Pitfall 10: Falling in Love with a Clue

Sometimes a clue is beautifully written.

Sometimes it is genuinely interesting.

Neither guarantees that it belongs in the dataset.

Editors should never hesitate to remove a clue that:

- is ambiguous;
- duplicates another clue;
- disrupts progression;
- relies on speculation; or
- weakens gameplay.

Every clue should earn its place.

---



# Editorial Case Studies

The following examples illustrate how editorial review improves dataset quality.

### Sarah

Original idea:

> I was the only woman whose age at death was recorded.

Improved version:

> I am the only woman whose age at death is explicitly recorded in the Bible.

The revised clue removes possible ambiguity while remaining concise.

---



### Terah

Original idea:

> I travelled toward Canaan.

Improved version:

> I began the journey to Canaan, but my son completed it.

The revised clue transforms a simple fact into a memorable narrative observation.

---



### Shem

Original idea:

> I outlived Abraham.

Rejected.

Chronological review showed this conclusion was incorrect.

Replacement:

> Abraham was the ninth generation in line from me.

Editorial review strengthened both the accuracy and the gameplay.

---



### Zipporah

Original idea:

> I missed the ten plagues.

Rejected.

Scripture does not establish this beyond reasonable doubt.

Replacement:

> I called my husband a bridegroom of blood.

The revised clue rests entirely on explicit biblical evidence.

---



### Zelophehad

Original idea:

> My descendants became the Moabites and Ammonites.

Rejected.

Improved version:

> My grandson became the father of both the Moabites and the Ammonites.

The revision is more precise while preserving the intended difficulty.

---


# The Editor's Mindset

Good contributors ask:

> "Can I write another clue?"

Good editors ask:

> "Should this clue exist at all?"

Every accepted clue increases the quality of the dataset.

Every rejected clue protects it.

Learning to remove weak clues is one of the defining skills of a valuable editor of the Whoami dataset.

---



## Key Takeaways

- Verify every assumption.
- Avoid ambiguity whenever possible.
- Distinguish synthesis from speculation.
- Specific clues outperform generic ones.
- Difficulty comes from reasoning, not obscurity.
- Be willing to discard good clues in pursuit of great datasets.

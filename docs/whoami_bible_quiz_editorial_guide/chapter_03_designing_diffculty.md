# 3. Designing Difficulty

One of the most common mistakes when creating datasets is misunderstanding what makes a clue difficult.

A clue is **not** difficult only because it comes from an obscure verse.

A clue is **not** difficult only because few people know it.

A clue is difficult because it leaves multiple reasonable possibilities until the player recognises the connection.

The purpose of difficulty is to create satisfying progression throughout a game—not simply to test obscure knowledge.

---

# Candidate Reduction

Every clue reduces the number of possible answers.

This is known as **candidate reduction**.

Imagine every Bible character as a possible answer.

The first clue removes many candidates.

The second removes more.

Each subsequent clue should continue narrowing the list until only one answer remains.

Difficulty is determined by **how effectively a clue narrows the candidate pool**, not by how obscure the supporting verse is.

This principle should guide every editorial decision.

---



# Easy

Easy clues should identify the character using their most widely recognised characteristics.

These clues often refer to:

- famous events
- well-known relationships
- iconic miracles
- defining moments

Examples:

> I built an ark.

> I was swallowed by a great fish.

> I led the Israelites out of Egypt.

These clues often identify the character immediately.

That is intentional.

Every dataset should begin by building player confidence.

---



# Medium

Medium clues move beyond the most famous facts while remaining reasonably familiar.

These clues often involve:

- close family relationships
- notable conversations
- secondary events
- remembered decisions

A player familiar with Scripture should recognise these clues without requiring detailed analysis.

---



# Hard

Hard clues reward familiarity with the biblical narrative.

They often require players to connect two or more passages or remember details that are less frequently discussed.

Examples include:

- less famous events
- indirect relationships
- recurring narrative themes
- supporting roles in significant stories

These clues should remain fair.

A careful Bible reader should still be able to reason towards the answer.

---



# Nightmare

Nightmare clues represent the highest editorial standard.

They should reward understanding—not obscurity.

Excellent Nightmare clues often require synthesis rather than recall.

These clues commonly involve:

- chronology
- genealogy
- narrative observation
- relationships between multiple passages
- patterns that emerge only after reading several texts together

For example:

> Abraham was the ninth generation in line from me.

This clue requires reconstructing a genealogy.

Nothing about it is obscure.

Its difficulty comes from the reasoning required.

Similarly:

> I was still alive when Sarah died.

This clue requires chronological synthesis across several passages.

Again, the difficulty comes from connecting Scripture rather than remembering a hidden fact.

---



## Editorial Insight

The strongest Nightmare clues often become a player's favourite clue after the answer is revealed.

Players should be able to say:

> "I never realised that..."

rather than:

> "How was I supposed to know that?"

---



# Obscure Does Not Mean Difficult

Contributors often assume that an obscure verse automatically produces a Hard or Nightmare clue.

This is incorrect.

For example:

> I owned a particular field.

If this fact appears in only one verse but has little gameplay value, it remains a poor clue regardless of its obscurity.

Likewise, a famous passage can produce an excellent Nightmare clue if it requires careful synthesis.

Always evaluate the reasoning required—not the popularity of the verse.

---



# Progressive Revelation

A dataset should feel like solving a puzzle.

Each clue should reveal slightly more information than the previous one.

Avoid sudden jumps in difficulty.

For example:

Easy:

> I was the wife of Moses.

Nightmare:

> I called someone a bridegroom of blood.

This skips several stages of progression.

Instead, build naturally:

Easy
→ wife of Moses

Medium
→ daughter of Jethro

Hard
→ reunited with Moses in the wilderness

Nightmare
→ my husband named one of our sons because he had become a foreign resident.

Each clue should make the picture a little clearer.

---



# Editorial Calibration

When assigning a difficulty, ask:

- How many reasonable candidates remain after reading this clue?
- Would most Bible readers immediately recognise it?
- Does the clue require connecting multiple passages?
- Does it reward understanding rather than memorisation?
- Does it fit naturally within the progression of the dataset?

If two clues feel equally difficult, prefer the simpler wording.

Difficulty should come from biblical knowledge—not from complicated language.

---



## Key Takeaways

- Difficulty is determined by candidate reduction.
- Obscure verses do not automatically produce difficult clues.
- Nightmare clues should reward synthesis.
- Progression is more important than absolute difficulty.
- Players should feel challenged, not tricked.

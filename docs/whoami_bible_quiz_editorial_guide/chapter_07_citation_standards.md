# 7. Citation Standards

Every clue in a WhoAmI dataset must be supported by Scripture.

Citations are not merely references for curious readers—they are the evidence that allows reviewers to verify every clue for accuracy.

A clue without a supporting citation should never be accepted.

---

# Why Citations Matter

Good citations allow reviewers to answer three questions quickly:

1. Is the clue true?
2. Does Scripture support the wording?
3. Is the cited passage sufficient?

Well-chosen citations make the editorial process faster and more reliable.

---

# Cite the Supporting Passage

Always cite the passage that directly supports the clue.

Good:

> I built an ark.
>
> Genesis 6:14

Poor:

> I built an ark.
>
> Genesis 5–9

The first citation points directly to the supporting evidence.

The second requires unnecessary searching.

Prefer the smallest passage that fully supports the clue.

---

# Cite Every Supporting Passage

Some clues require more than one passage.

Whenever multiple passages are needed to establish a fact, cite all of them.

Example:

> Abraham was the ninth generation in line from me.

This conclusion requires multiple genealogical passages.

Each relevant passage should be cited.

Never expect reviewers to reconstruct missing evidence.

---

# Cite the Evidence, Not the Conclusion

Some conclusions are derived from Scripture rather than stated directly.

In these cases, cite the passages that allow the conclusion to be reached.

Do not cite external chronologies, study notes, commentaries, or reference works.

The Bible itself should provide the evidence.

---

# Prefer Primary Evidence

For example, if a clue says:

> I had five daughters.

You could cite Joshua 17 where they're named again, or Numbers 26 where they're first listed.

The editorial preference should be to cite the passage that most directly establishes the fact—the "primary evidence"—unless a later passage is required for context.

That keeps citations consistent across contributors and avoids everyone picking different verses for the same fact.

---

# Avoid Unnecessary Citations

Do not include citations that do not contribute to verifying the clue.

Poor:

> I built an ark.
>
> Genesis 5:32
> Genesis 6:14
> Genesis 7:1
> Genesis 8:18
> Genesis 9:29

Only Genesis 6:14 directly supports the statement.

Additional citations add noise without increasing confidence.

---

# Whole Chapters vs. Individual Verses

Whenever practical, cite individual verses.

Acceptable:

Genesis 6:14

Preferable to:

Genesis 6

However, if a clue depends on the flow of an entire narrative, a short passage range may be more appropriate.

Example:

Genesis 22:1–19

The goal is clarity rather than absolute brevity.

---

# Multiple Verse Ranges

Some clues require information from separate parts of Scripture.

List each reference individually.

Example:

Genesis 5:3–32

Genesis 11:10–26

Genesis 23:1

This makes the reasoning transparent.

---

# Direct Quotations

Direct quotations should be used sparingly.

Most clues should summarise biblical events rather than quoting Scripture verbatim.

When quoting directly:

- use the chosen translation consistently;
- reproduce the wording accurately;
- ensure the quotation meaningfully improves the clue.

Avoid quoting simply because a memorable verse exists.

---

# Citation and Difficulty

Difficulty should never come from hiding supporting references.

Every clue should remain fully verifiable regardless of whether it is Easy or Nightmare.

A Nightmare clue may require more reasoning.

It should never require weaker evidence.

---

# Editorial Checklist

Before approving a citation, ask:

- Does this passage directly support the clue?
- Have all necessary passages been cited?
- Can another reviewer verify the clue quickly?
- Is every citation actually necessary?
- Does the evidence come from Scripture alone?

---

## Key Takeaways

- Every clue requires supporting Scripture.
- Cite the evidence, not the conclusion.
- Use the smallest passage that fully supports the clue.
- Include every passage required for synthesis.
- Avoid unnecessary references.
- Good citations make good reviews possible.

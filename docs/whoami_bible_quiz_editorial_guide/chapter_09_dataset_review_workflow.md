# 9. Dataset Review Workflow

Creating a high-quality dataset is an iterative process.

Very few datasets are publication-ready after the first draft.

The purpose of editorial review is not to criticise contributors, but to improve the final experience for players.

Every review should make the dataset more accurate, more enjoyable, and easier to maintain.

---

# The Review Principles

Reviewers should approach every dataset with three goals:

1. Verify biblical accuracy.
2. Improve gameplay.
3. Maintain consistency across the project.

A clue may be perfectly accurate but still require revision if it is ambiguous, repetitive, or poorly placed within the difficulty progression.

---

# Stage 1: Completeness Review

Before examining individual clues, verify that the dataset itself is complete.

Check that it includes:

- the correct character or place name;
- aliases (where applicable);
- the required number of clues;
- difficulty assignments;
- Scripture citations;
- metadata fields required by the schema.

Datasets that are incomplete should be returned before detailed review begins.

---

# Stage 2: Biblical Accuracy Review

Review each clue independently.

Ask:

- Is this statement true?
- Do the cited passages support it?
- Does the wording accurately reflect Scripture?
- Is any part of the clue based on assumption rather than evidence?

This stage focuses only on correctness.

Gameplay considerations come later.

---

# Stage 3: Difficulty Review

Read the clues in order without looking at the answer.

Ask yourself:

- Does the progression feel natural?
- Does each clue reduce the candidate pool?
- Does the difficulty steadily increase?
- Are any clues obviously misplaced?

If a Nightmare clue feels easier than a Medium clue, reconsider the ordering.

---

# Stage 4: Variety Review

Look at the clue types.

Avoid datasets where every clue is:

- a relationship;
- an event;
- a genealogy;
- a quotation.

Strong datasets combine multiple clue types.

Variety creates a richer and more engaging experience.

---

# Stage 5: Redundancy Review

Identify clues that communicate essentially the same information.

Examples:

> I was the father of Isaac.

> Isaac was my son.

Only one should remain.

Every accepted clue should contribute something unique.

---

# Stage 6: Ambiguity Review

This is one of the most important stages.

Ask:

> Could another careful Bible reader reasonably disagree with this clue?

If yes, revise or remove it.

Remember:

The burden of proof belongs to the contributor.

When certainty cannot be demonstrated from Scripture, the clue should not be included.

---

# Stage 7: Enjoyment Review

Finally, forget that you are an editor.

Become a player.

Read the clues from beginning to end.

Ask:

- Would this be enjoyable to guess?
- Does each clue reveal something interesting?
- Is there a satisfying "Aha!" moment?
- Does the answer feel earned?

Editorial accuracy alone does not create a great game.

Enjoyment matters just as much.

---

# Handling Review Feedback

Editorial feedback should focus on improving the dataset rather than criticising the contributor.

Prefer comments such as:

> This clue may be ambiguous because...

> Could this be supported with a stronger citation?

> I wonder if this clue would work better as a Hard clue.

Avoid comments such as:

> This is wrong.

> Bad clue.

Explain the reasoning behind suggested changes whenever practical.

The goal is to build shared understanding, not simply approve or reject content.

---

# When to Reject a Clue

A clue should normally be rejected if it:

- cannot be demonstrated from Scripture;
- depends on disputed interpretation;
- duplicates another clue;
- weakens the difficulty progression;
- adds little gameplay value;
- requires unnecessary explanation;
- is supported by weak or incomplete citations.

Removing one weak clue often improves the entire dataset.

---

# When Is a Dataset Ready?

A dataset is ready for publication when:

- every clue is scripturally supported;
- every citation has been verified;
- the progression feels natural;
- clue types are well balanced;
- no obvious ambiguity remains;
- reviewers would enjoy playing it themselves.

Perfection is rarely achievable.

Consistency, accuracy, and enjoyable gameplay are the editorial standard.

---

# Editorial Checklist

Before approving a dataset, confirm:

- ✓ All metadata is complete.
- ✓ Every clue is accurate.
- ✓ Every clue has sufficient citations.
- ✓ Difficulty progression feels natural.
- ✓ Clue types are varied.
- ✓ No duplicate information remains.
- ✓ Ambiguous clues have been removed.
- ✓ The dataset is enjoyable to play.

Only after all of these checks have been completed should the dataset be approved.

---

## Editorial Insight

A review is successful when the contributor feels that the dataset became better—not merely different.

The best reviews preserve the contributor's ideas while improving their clarity, accuracy, and gameplay value.

Editors are not gatekeepers.

They are collaborators whose responsibility is to help every dataset reach its highest standard.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Review systematically, not randomly.
- Separate accuracy from gameplay during review.
- Verify every clue before considering its difficulty.
- Remove weak clues, even if they are interesting.
- Good feedback teaches as well as corrects.
- Editorial review is a collaborative process.

# 6. Writing Great Clues

Understanding the editorial principles is only the first step.

This chapter explains how to write clues that are enjoyable to play, consistent in quality, and faithful to Scripture.

Think of these guidelines as the practical application of the principles established in previous chapters.

---

# Write From the Character's Perspective

Every clue should be written in the first person.

The player should feel as though the biblical character is speaking directly to them.

Good:

> I built an ark.

> I became the father of Isaac.

> I was thrown into a lions' pit.

Avoid:

> He built an ark.

> Moses' wife was me.

> This person became king.

Maintain the illusion throughout the entire dataset.

---

# One Clue, One Idea

Each clue should communicate a single memorable fact.

Avoid combining multiple unrelated ideas into one clue.

Poor:

> I built an ark, survived the Flood, and became the father of many nations.

Good:

> I built an ark.

Good:

> My family survived the Flood with me.

Breaking information into separate clues creates a smoother progression throughout the game.

---

# Prefer Natural Language

Write as though the character is telling their own story.

Avoid wording that sounds like a database entry.

Poor:

> My spouse was Zipporah.

Better:

> I married Zipporah.

Poor:

> Tribe affiliation: Judah.

Better:

> I belonged to the tribe of Judah.

Natural language makes clues more enjoyable to read without sacrificing accuracy.

---

# Use Biblical Structure Purposefully

Most clues should describe biblical people, events, and statements rather than
merely identifying where material appears.

Weak structural clues include:

> My story appears in Genesis.

> I was first mentioned in Exodus.

> My account spans three chapters.

These reveal little of interest and usually test only basic document layout.

However, biblical structure is not forbidden. A structural or whole-Bible clue
may be useful when it communicates a distinctive, meaningful observation that
rewards broad knowledge of Scripture.

Examples include:

> The first recorded murder in Scripture was committed by me.

> I am the only woman whose age at death is explicitly recorded in the Bible.

Such clues should be retained only when:

- the observation is demonstrably correct across Scripture;
- the underlying event is supported by an appropriate citation;
- the wording contributes meaningful identifying information;
- it does more than state a book, chapter, list position, or number of verses;
- it remains natural from the character's perspective.

Because exhaustive claims cannot always be proved by one short passage, their
accuracy must be checked against the whole Bible. The cited passage should still
directly support the event or fact on which the whole-Bible observation rests.

---

# Show, Don't Explain

Whenever possible, describe what happened rather than explaining its significance.

Poor:

> I demonstrated great faith.

Better:

> I left my homeland without knowing where I was going.

The second clue allows players to recognise the character naturally rather than being told how to interpret them.

---

# Avoid Redundant Clues

Every clue should add something new.

Avoid repeating the same fact in different words.

Example:

> I was the father of Isaac.

> Isaac was my son.

Only one of these is necessary.

Likewise:

> I was the wife of Moses.

> Moses was my husband.

Duplicate clues reduce the overall quality of the dataset.

---

# Avoid Alias Clues

Aliases should generally not become clues.

If an alternative name already exists in the dataset metadata, repeating it as a clue adds little gameplay value.

Example:

Metadata:

```
aliases:
- Sarai
```

Weak clue:

> I was once called Sarai.

Reserve aliases for metadata unless the name change itself plays a significant role in the biblical narrative.

---

# Use Specific Language

Specific clues are almost always stronger than general ones.

Weak:

> I had children.

Better:

> I had five daughters.

Weak:

> I travelled.

Better:

> I fled from Egypt to Midian.

Specificity naturally reduces the candidate pool.

---

# Avoid Artificial Complexity

A clue should never become difficult because of awkward wording.

Players should struggle with the biblical knowledge—not the sentence.

Poor:

> Prior to the commencement of my subsequent migration...

Better:

> I fled to Midian.

Simple language almost always produces better gameplay.

---

# Preserve the Mystery

Avoid including the answer, obvious aliases, or uniquely identifying titles too early.

Example:

Easy:

> I became king after Saul.

Nightmare:

> I wrote many psalms.

This progression is backwards.

Instead:

Easy:

> I defeated Goliath.

Medium:

> I became king after Saul.

Hard:

> I spared my enemy's life inside a cave.

Nightmare:

> I refused to harm Jehovah's anointed.

Each clue should reveal only enough information for its intended difficulty.

---

# Respect the Player

Never attempt to trick the player.

Avoid clues that rely on:

- technicalities
- unusual wording
- misleading phrasing
- hidden exceptions

The challenge should come entirely from Scripture.

Players should lose because they missed a biblical connection—not because they misunderstood the clue.

---

# Editorial Checklist

Before accepting any clue, ask yourself:

- Is it accurate?
- Is it memorable?
- Is it distinctive?
- Does it sound natural?
- Does it introduce new information?
- Is it appropriate for its difficulty?
- Could it reasonably be misunderstood?
- Would I enjoy guessing it?

If several answers are "no", rewrite the clue.

Good editors rewrite far more clues than they publish.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Write in the first person.
- Express one idea per clue.
- Use natural, conversational language.
- Describe events rather than interpretations.
- Avoid duplicates and metadata-based clues.
- Keep wording simple.
- Challenge biblical knowledge—not reading comprehension.
- Revision is an essential part of the editorial process.

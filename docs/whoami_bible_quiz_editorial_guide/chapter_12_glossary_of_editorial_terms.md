# 12. Glossary of Editorial Terms

This glossary defines the editorial terminology used throughout the WhoAmI Bible Quiz Editorial Guide.

These definitions establish a shared vocabulary for contributors, reviewers, and AI-assisted workflows.

---

## Aha! Moment

The moment a player understands why a clue fits the correct answer.

The best clues produce satisfaction through recognition rather than surprise.

Example:

> "I never realised that before."

not

> "How was I supposed to know that?"

---

## Alias

An alternative biblical name for the same person or place.

Aliases belong primarily in dataset metadata rather than in clues unless the name change itself is historically or narratively significant.

Examples:

- Abram → Abraham
- Sarai → Sarah
- Hoshea → Joshua

---

## Ambiguity

A situation where a clue supports more than one reasonable interpretation or more than one reasonable answer.

Ambiguity should be eliminated wherever possible.

---

## Candidate Reduction

The process by which each clue reduces the number of possible answers available to the player.

Strong clues significantly narrow the candidate pool.

Difficulty is largely determined by the rate of candidate reduction.

---

## Characteristic Clue

A clue describing a defining trait, role, title, occupation, or personal attribute of a character.

Example:

> I interpreted dreams.

---

## Chronological Clue

A clue that requires reasoning about dates, ages, lifespans, or the sequence of biblical events.

Example:

> I was still alive when Sarah died.

---

## Citation

The Scripture reference supporting a clue.

Every clue must be supported by one or more citations that allow the statement to be independently verified.

---

## Direct Clue

A clue based directly on a statement or event explicitly recorded in Scripture.

Example:

> I built an ark.

---

## Distinctive Clue

A clue that meaningfully separates one candidate from many others.

Distinctiveness is one of the defining characteristics of a high-quality clue.

---

## Editorial Neutrality

The practice of describing what Scripture records without introducing theological interpretation, doctrinal conclusions, or denominational assumptions.

Editorial neutrality promotes consistency and allows datasets to be used by a broad audience.

---

## Gameplay Value

The contribution a clue makes to the player's enjoyment.

Gameplay value includes:

- memorability;
- fairness;
- candidate reduction;
- satisfaction when the answer is revealed.

Interesting facts do not automatically provide good gameplay.

---

## Genealogical Clue

A clue requiring players to reason through biblical family relationships.

Example:

> Abraham was the ninth generation in line from me.

---

## Golden Rule

The central principle governing narrative inference.

> A clue may be inferred only if the cited passages lead to a single reasonable conclusion.

Whenever multiple reasonable conclusions remain possible, the clue should be revised or rejected.

---

## Narrative Clue

A clue describing events within the biblical story rather than isolated facts.

Narrative clues reward familiarity with Scripture as a continuous account.

---

## Narrative Inference

A conclusion derived from multiple biblical passages or from the natural flow of a narrative.

Narrative inference is acceptable only when Scripture supports one reasonable conclusion.

---

## Nightmare Clue

The highest level of clue difficulty.

Nightmare clues reward synthesis, reasoning, chronology, genealogy, or deep narrative understanding rather than obscure memorisation.

---

## Primary Evidence

The Scripture passage that most directly establishes a clue.

Contributors should prefer citing primary evidence wherever possible.

---

## Progression

The gradual increase in difficulty throughout a dataset.

Every successive clue should reveal additional information while preserving the puzzle.

---

## Relationship Clue

A clue identifying a character through family or other significant relationships.

Examples include parents, children, spouses, tribes, or descendants.

---

## Redundant Clue

A clue that repeats information already communicated elsewhere in the dataset.

Redundant clues reduce gameplay value and should normally be removed.

---

## Speculation

A conclusion that goes beyond what Scripture establishes.

Speculation has no place in WhoAmI datasets.

When evidence is uncertain, contributors should stay closer to the biblical text.

---

## Synthesis

The process of combining multiple scriptural facts into a single objective conclusion.

Unlike interpretation, synthesis does not add meaning beyond what Scripture already establishes.

---

## Translation Standard

The chosen Bible translation used for direct quotations, names, and citations within a dataset.

The editorial principles in this guide remain applicable regardless of the translation selected.

---

## Worked Example

An annotated editorial example demonstrating how clues are reviewed, revised, or rejected.

Worked examples illustrate editorial reasoning rather than prescribing the only acceptable solution.

---

# Closing Thoughts

Good Bible quizzes test memory.

Great Bible quizzes reward understanding.

The goal of the WhoAmI Bible Quiz is not to ask the hardest questions or uncover the most obscure facts.

It is to help players engage more deeply with Scripture by recognising people, places, and events through careful observation, thoughtful reasoning, and growing familiarity with the biblical narrative.

Every clue should reflect that purpose.

Every review should strengthen it.

Every dataset should invite players to discover something they had never noticed before.

If contributors consistently apply the principles in this guide, they will create datasets that are scripturally faithful, enjoyable to play, and rewarding to explore for years to come.

# 10. Worked Examples

The previous chapters explained the principles behind good clue writing.

This chapter demonstrates those principles in practice.

Each example is drawn from real editorial decisions made during the development of the WhoAmI datasets.

The purpose is not to present the only correct solution, but to illustrate how contributors and reviewers should think through difficult editorial decisions.

---

# Example 1: Sarah

### Original Clue

> I was the only woman whose age at death was recorded.

### Editorial Review

The clue is distinctive and scripturally supported.

However, the wording could be interpreted as referring only to women mentioned in Genesis or a particular period of biblical history.

Although most readers would understand the intended meaning, editorial standards favour removing unnecessary ambiguity.

### Final Version

> I am the only woman whose exact age at death was recorded in the Bible.

### Why It Works

- More precise.
- Removes possible misunderstanding.
- Retains the same gameplay value.

---

# Example 2: Terah

### Original Clue

> I travelled toward Canaan.

### Editorial Review

The clue is accurate but generic.

Many biblical figures travelled.

It contributes little to identifying the character.

### Final Version

> I began the journey to Canaan, but my son completed it.

### Why It Works

- Tells part of a story.
- Rewards narrative knowledge.
- More memorable.
- Stronger candidate reduction.

---

# Example 3: Shem

### Original Clue

> I outlived Abraham.

### Editorial Review

Chronological verification showed this was incorrect.

The clue was rejected.

Rather than searching for another chronological fact, the review identified a stronger genealogical observation.

### Final Version

> Abraham was the ninth generation in line from me.

### Why It Works

- Completely supported by Scripture.
- Rewards synthesis.
- Makes an excellent Nightmare clue.
- Requires reasoning rather than obscure knowledge.

---

# Example 4: Zipporah

### Original Clue

> I missed the ten plagues.

### Editorial Review

Although plausible, Scripture never establishes exactly when Moses sent Zipporah back to Midian.

Multiple timelines remain possible.

The clue depends on assumption rather than demonstration.

It was rejected.

### Final Version

> I called my husband a bridegroom of blood.

### Why It Works

- Explicitly recorded.
- Distinctive.
- Memorable.
- Removes unnecessary speculation.

---

# Example 5: Zelophehad

### Original Clue

> My descendants became the Moabites and Ammonites.

### Editorial Review

The wording is technically true but imprecise.

The relationship is more specific.

### Final Version

> My grandson became the father of both the Moabites and the Ammonites.

### Why It Works

- Greater precision.
- Stronger gameplay.
- Better genealogy.
- Easier to verify.

---

# Lessons from These Examples

Notice that none of the final clues became better because they were made more complicated.

Instead, they became better because they became:

- more accurate;
- more precise;
- more distinctive;
- more memorable; or
- more enjoyable to solve.

This is the goal of every editorial review.

---

## Editorial Insight

One of the recurring themes throughout these examples is restraint.

In every case, the review process resisted the temptation to include an interesting clue simply because it seemed plausible.

Instead, the guiding question was always:

> **"Can this clue be demonstrated from Scripture beyond reasonable doubt?"**

That discipline is one of the defining characteristics of the WhoAmI editorial process.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Editorial review refines ideas rather than replacing them.
- Precision almost always improves gameplay.
- Good clues survive careful scrutiny.
- Great clues become stronger through revision.
- Real examples are often the best teachers.
